## 1.6 The Hiatus (Pause Between Two Sounds)

In English, we naturally run words together so that the final consonant of one word acts as a bridge to the initial vowel of the next word (for example, *"for ages"* sounds like *"fo-rages"*). 

**This never happens in German!** Words (or root syllables inside a word) beginning with a vowel are always preceded by a **hiatus** (also known as a glottal stop, marked with `*` in the book)—a crisp, brief pause between the two sounds. In English, you can hear this exact pause in *"Uh * oh!"*, *"re-*elect"*, or *"co-*operate"*.

### Book Examples of the Hiatus (`*`)

| German Word / Phrase | English Comparison | Meaning | Example Sentence for Memory |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **mach \* aus** | *(Do not blend like "ma-chaus")* | switch off! | ***Mach \* aus** das Licht, bitte!* (Switch off the light, please!) |
| **im \* Auge** | *(Do not blend like "i-mauge")* | in the eye | *Ich habe etwas **im \* Auge**.* (I have something in my eye.) |
| **hau \* ab** | Compare with English *"How are you?"* | clear off! / beat it! | ***Hau \* ab**, ich will Ruhe haben!* (Clear off, I want some peace!) |
| **Vor\*arbeiter** | Compare with English *"for ages"* | foreman | *Der **Vor\*arbeiter** hilft uns heute.* (The foreman is helping us today.) |
| **miss\*achten** | Pause after prefix *miss-* | to disregard / ignore | *Man darf das Gesetz nicht **miss\*achten**.* (One must not disregard the law.) |
| **ge\*einigt** | Pause between the two *e*'s | agreed / united | *Wir haben uns endlich **ge\*einigt**.* (We have finally agreed.) |
| **im \* Ofen** | Pause after *im* | in the oven | *Das Brot ist schon **im \* Ofen**.* (The bread is already in the oven.) |
| **würde \* ich \* auch** | Pause before *ich* and *auch* | I would too | *Das **würde \* ich \* auch** sagen!* (I would say that too!) |

## 1.7 Word Stress

The rhythm of a language depends heavily on where the stress falls in words and sentences. In German, **the stress usually falls on the first syllable**, though there are important rules and exceptions to learn. (Stressed syllables are shown in **bold** below.)

### 1. The Prefix `un-` is ALWAYS Stressed
Whereas English words starting with *un-* (like *un**hap**py* or *un**friend**ly*) leave the prefix unstressed, German puts the main stress **directly on the `un-`**:

| German Word (Stress on **un-**) | Meaning | Example Sentence for Memory |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **un**glücklich | unhappy / unlucky | *Er war gestern sehr **un**glücklich.* (He was very unhappy yesterday.) |
| **un**erfahren | inexperienced | *Der junge Fahrer ist noch **un**erfahren.* (The young driver is still inexperienced.) |
| **un**freundlich | unfriendly | *Der Kellner war leider **un**freundlich.* (The waiter was unfortunately unfriendly.) |
| **un**geduldig | impatient | *Warum bist du immer so **un**geduldig?* (Why are you always so impatient?) |

---

### 2. Compound Words: Stress the First Word Unit
German frequently creates long compound words out of smaller word units. In these compound words, the main stress falls on the **stressed syllable of the very first word unit**:

| Compound Word | Word Breakdown | Meaning | Example Sentence for Memory |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **krei**deweiß | **krei**de \| weiß | chalk-white (deadly pale) | *Sein Gesicht wurde plötzlich **krei**deweiß.* (His face suddenly turned chalk-white.) |
| **Plat**tenspieler | **Plat**ten \| spieler | record player | *Der alte **Plat**tenspieler funktioniert noch.* (The old record player still works.) |
| **Bril**lenetui | **Bril**len \| etui | glasses case | *Mein **Bril**lenetui liegt auf dem Tisch.* (My glasses case is lying on the table.) |
| **Stu**den**tenwohnheim | Stu**den**ten \| wohn \| heim | student hall of residence / dorm | *Sie wohnt in einem **Stu**den**tenwohnheim.* (She lives in a student dorm—note that *Stu**den**ten* is a foreign-origin word stressed on *-den-*!) |

---

### 3. Exceptions: Where Stress is NOT on the First Syllable

There are three main categories of words where the stress falls later in the word:

#### A. Words of Foreign Origin (Often ending in *-ieren*, *-ine*, *-et*, *-ell*)
* **kontrol**lie**ren** (to check) $\rightarrow$ *Die Polizei muss die Pässe **kontrol**lie**ren.* (The police have to check the passports.)
* **telefo**nie**ren** (to phone) $\rightarrow$ *Wir **telefo**nie**ren jeden Sonntag.* (We talk on the phone every Sunday.)
* **Ma**schi**ne** (machine) $\rightarrow$ *Diese **Ma**schi**ne ist ganz neu.* (This machine is brand new.)
* **Pa**ket** (parcel/package) $\rightarrow$ *Ein **Pa**ket für dich ist da!* (A parcel for you is here!)
* **offizi**ell** (official) $\rightarrow$ *Das Ergebnis ist jetzt **offizi**ell.* (The result is now official.)

#### B. Verbs with Inseparable Unstressed Prefixes (`be-`, `miss-`, `ver-`, `ge-`, `er-`, `ent-`, `zer-`)
* *be|***spre**chen (to discuss) $\rightarrow$ *Wir müssen das Problem *be|***spre**chen.* (We must discuss the problem.)
* *miss|***brau**chen (to misuse/abuse) $\rightarrow$ *Man soll Vertrauen nicht *miss|***brau**chen.* (One should not abuse trust.)
* *ver|***ra**ten (to betray/give away) $\rightarrow$ *Bitte *ver|***ra**te mein Geheimnis nicht!* (Please don't give away my secret!)
* *ge|***lin**gen (to succeed) $\rightarrow$ *Der Kuchen wird bestimmt *ge|***lin**gen.* (The cake will definitely succeed.)
* *er|***rö**ten (to blush) $\rightarrow$ *Er musste vor Scham *er|***rö**ten.* (He had to blush with embarrassment.)

#### C. Common Short Words & `da-` / `wo-` Combinations
* **je**doch** (however), **so**gar** (even), **in**zwi**schen (meanwhile)
* **da**mit** (with it / so that), **da**zu** (to it / in addition), **da**ne**ben (next to it), **wo**rauf** (on what / whereupon)
* **Example Sentence:** ***In**zwi**schen hat er **so**gar** ein Auto gekauft, **da**mit** er zur Arbeit fahren kann.* (Meanwhile he has even bought a car so that he can drive to work.)

## 1.8 German Punctuation

German punctuation follows strict grammatical rules rather than just indicating pauses for breath:

### 1. Commas Separate All Clauses
In English, we use commas or omit them to change the meaning of a relative clause:
* *My sister, who hates noise, ended up staying in a hotel next to a night club.* (Has commas)
* *The woman who rang yesterday was a friend of my sister's.* (No commas in English)

In German, **even the second sentence MUST have commas** before and after the subordinate clause (`..., die gestern anrief, ...`) because German grammar requires every clause with its own conjugated verb to be separated by a comma!
* **German Example:** *Die Frau**,** die gestern anrief**,** war eine Freundin meiner Schwester.*

### 2. Quotation Marks, Colons, and Exclamation Marks
* **Quoted Speech:** German uses low opening quotation marks and high closing quotation marks: **„Hallo!“** (covered in detail in Week 13).
* **Colons (`:`):** Used more frequently than in English to introduce direct speech, explanations, or short inserts in the middle of sentences.
* **Exclamation Marks (`!`):** Used much more frequently in German than in English—every command (imperative), warning, or greeting like *Guten Tag!* or *Kommen Sie sofort!* takes an exclamation mark.

## 🗣️ Mini Speaking Drill (Read Aloud!)
Read these 5 sentences aloud, practicing the **hiatus pause (`*`)**, **word stress (bold)**, and **clause commas**:

1. **Mach \* aus** das Radio, **in**zwi**schen **telefo**nie**re \* ich!  
   *(Switch off the radio, meanwhile I am on the phone!)*
2. Der **Vor\*arbeiter** war **un**geduldig und **un**freundlich.  
   *(The foreman was impatient and unfriendly.)*
3. Das **Pa**ket** liegt **im \* Ofen**? Das **würde \* ich \* auch** komisch finden!  
   *(The parcel is lying in the oven? I would find that strange too!)*
4. Wir haben uns **ge\*einigt**, dass wir die **Ma**schi**ne **kontrol**lie**ren.  
   *(We agreed that we will check the machine.)*
5. Mein **Bril**lenetui ist im **Stu**den**tenwohnheim, **da**ne**ben steht der **Plat**tenspieler.  
   *(My glasses case is in the student dorm; next to it stands the record player.)*
